Build a round-robin arbiter

Two requesters want one shared resource, such as a memory. The arbiter gives it to at most one of them in each clock cycle, and when both keep asking it alternates, so neither of them starves. You build it from four CMOS chips: a 4011 NAND, a 4081 AND, a 4071 OR and a 4013 D flip-flop.

1 board830 points, one 8-LED module
Before you start, do the getting-started steps: how the breadboard is connected inside, the power module set to 5 V, how a DIP chip sits across the gap (notch on the left, pin 1 bottom-left), and how a button module connects with three male-female jumpers.

How it works

The flip-flop stores one bit, L: who got the last grant. L = 0 means requester 0, L = 1 means requester 1. If only one requester asks, it gets the grant. If both ask, the one that did not get the last grant wins. At every rising clock edge the flip-flop takes the new value of L.

g0 = r0 AND NOT (r1 AND NOT L)
g1 = r1 AND NOT (r0 AND L)
next L = g1 OR (L AND NOT r0)

Read the first line as: requester 0 gets the grant when it asks, unless requester 1 also asks and it is requester 1's turn (L = 0). The rule for the next L comes from "grant 1 now: L becomes 1; grant 0 now: L becomes 0; nobody asks: L stays". When L = 1, grant 0 happens exactly when r0 asks, so this rule shrinks to the third line.

The trick that saves a chip. The NAND gate N2 already computes y = NOT (r0 AND L) for grant 1. AND it with L and you get L AND y = L AND NOT r0, the term the next L needs. So there is no inverter chip: the two NANDs and the flip-flop's Q output give every NOT the circuit needs.

Schematic 2: connections between the chips

Each chip is drawn from above with the notch on the left, the way it sits on the breadboard: pins 1–7 along the bottom, pins 8–14 along the top from right to left. Columns are numbered from the power module end; rows a–e are the upper half and f–j the lower half, as in the drawing (some boards letter them the other way round). A hole name such as 26h means column 26, row h. The five holes of one column in one half are connected, so a wire can go into any free hole of the right column. The wires are drawn flat with right angles so that you can follow them; real Dupont jumpers arch over the board and the chips, and that is fine.

Build it step by step

  1. Power module. Plug it onto the left end of the board (columns 1–6) with + into the + rails. Set both jumpers to 5 V. Do not connect the cable yet.
  2. Chips. Insert them across the gap, notch on the left: 4013 in columns 12–18, 4011 in 22–28, 4081 in 32–38, 4071 in 42–48. Pin 1 of each chip is in row f of its first column: 12f, 22f, 32f, 42f.
  3. Power wires, 8. Pin 14 of each chip (row a of its first column) to the top + rail, pin 7 (row j of its last column) to the bottom − rail.
  4. Tie-offs, 17. Unused inputs of the gate chips: top row to the top + rail, bottom row to the bottom − rail. The four inputs of the unused flip-flop (4013 pins 8–11, columns 15–18, row a) go to the top − rail, over the + rail. Pin 6 of the 4013 (S of the flip-flop we use, 17j) goes to the bottom − rail.
  5. Check the power. Connect the 9 V USB-DC cable for a moment: the power module LED lights and no chip gets warm. Disconnect it again.
  6. Signal wires, 15. Follow the checklist below and tick each wire off. Using the same colours as the drawing makes checking easier, but any colour works.
  7. LED module. Its header goes into row g, columns 53–61, with GND in column 53; the board of the module lies over the breadboard. Connect 53j to the bottom − rail.
  8. Modules, 12 male-female jumpers. Each module: + to the bottom + rail, − to the bottom − rail, OUT to its column: clock to 14j, reset to 19j (a short wire takes it on to pin 4 at 15i), request 0 to 23j, request 1 to 27j. Turn the potentiometer of the 555 module to about 1 tick per second.
  9. Power on and run the tests.

Test it

LED 1 shows grant g0, LED 2 grant g1 and LED 4 the flip-flop L.

Do thisYou should see
Press and release resetLED 4 goes off: L = 0, requester 0 had the last turn.
Press no buttonsLEDs 1 and 2 stay off; LED 4 keeps its value through the clock ticks.
Hold request 0LED 1 lights at once: the gates do not wait for the clock. LED 4 is off after the next tick.
Hold request 1LED 2 lights at once; LED 4 turns on at the next tick.
Press reset, then hold bothLED 2 lights first (it is requester 1's turn). After that LEDs 1 and 2 take turns, one per clock tick, and LED 4 toggles on every tick. Never both at once: that is the round robin.
Hold request 0 for a few ticks, then also press request 1The grant moves to requester 1 at once, because requester 0 had the last turn. This arbiter decides again in every cycle.

If something does not work

  • Nothing lights: check the power module LED, both jumpers at 5 V and the 9 V cable, as in the getting-started steps.
  • LEDs flicker or change by themselves: an input floats. Count the tie-offs (17) and check the − wire of every button module.
  • LED 4 never changes: look at the clock: wire 14j, the D wire 44j → 16j and the reset path 19j → 19i → 15i. The reset button must give 0 when it is not pressed, and pin 6 (17j) must go to −.
  • Both grants light at once: x or y is wired wrong: compare 25g → 32g and 24j → 37j with the checklist.
  • A chip gets hot: it is in backwards. Unplug the power at once.
  • Find a fault with a probe: put the single 5V LED with its long leg into a column and its short leg into the − rail, and compare what it shows with the truth table.

Next: the same arbiter in SystemVerilog

An engineer writes this block in a few lines, and synthesis turns it back into gates like the ones on your board. The if chain below is the rule "grant 0: L becomes 0; grant 1: L becomes 1", before simplification.

module round_robin_arbiter_2
(
    input              clk,
    input              rst,
    input        [1:0] req,
    output logic [1:0] gnt
);

    logic last;  // 1: the last grant went to requester 1

    assign gnt [0] = req [0] & ~ (req [1] & ~ last);
    assign gnt [1] = req [1] & ~ (req [0] &   last);

    always_ff @ (posedge clk or posedge rst)
        if (rst)
            last <= 1'b0;
        else if (gnt [0])
            last <= 1'b0;
        else if (gnt [1])
            last <= 1'b1;

endmodule

The reset is asynchronous, like the R input of the 4013. To run it on an FPGA board, use the Basics-Graphics-Music framework, which supports the same SystemVerilog module on dozens of boards: requests on two keys, grants on two LEDs.
