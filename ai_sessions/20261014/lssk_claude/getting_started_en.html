How to start: your first circuit on a breadboard

Five steps from Yuri Panchul's hand-drawn notes (July 2026), with the parts from the LSSK components sheet. At the end you have a CMOS logic gate on a breadboard, driven by two buttons and showing its output on an LED.

Why there are no resistors here. The kit uses components that make 1970s busywork unnecessary: 5V-tolerant LEDs and 8-LED modules with built-in current limiting, button modules with a built-in pull-down resistor, a breadboard power module that gives a stable 5 V, and an assembled 555 clock module. The only resistors you will use are for 7-segment displays, in later exercises.

1. How the breadboard is connected inside

  • The long rails along the top and bottom edges carry power. One is marked + (red line), the other − (blue or black line). All holes of one rail are connected along the board. Some boards split the rails in the middle: if a circuit at the far end does not work, check with an LED.
  • In the middle, every column of 5 holes is connected (the vertical strips in the drawing; the numbers 1, 5, 10, 15 … mark the columns). Anything you plug into the same column is connected.
  • The two halves are separated by a gap. A chip goes across the gap so that each of its pins gets its own column.

2. Connect the breadboard power supply

Plug the power module onto the end of the breadboard so that its pins go into the rails. Pay attention to + and −: the module's + pins must go into the + (red) rails.

  • + = VCC = +5 V. The name comes from "voltage at the common collector" of transistor circuits.
  • − = GND = ground = 0 V.
  • The module has a jumper or a switch for 3.3 V / 5 V on each side. Set both to 5 V.
  • Feed it from USB: either a USB cable into the module's own USB socket, or the 9 V USB-to-DC cable into its barrel jack.
A common mistake: a 5 V USB-to-DC cable in the barrel jack. The module's regulator drops it to about 3.5 V, not 5 V: LEDs are dim and circuits misbehave even with the switch at 5 V. Use the 9 V USB-to-DC cable.

3. Light an LED

  • LED = light-emitting diode. The long leg (anode) connects to +, VCC; the short leg (cathode) connects to −, GND.
  • We use special 5V-tolerant LEDs, so no resistor is needed.
5V-tolerant LEDs are different from the standard LEDs recommended by most tutorials since the 1970s. Do not use standard LEDs with these circuits: without a resistor they burn out.

Try it: plug the LED straight into the power rails, long leg into +, short leg into −. It lights. Turn it around: it stays dark, because a diode conducts in one direction only.

The 8-LED module works the same way: plug it in, connect its GND pin to −, and touch each input with a wire from +.

4. Insert a DIP chip

DIP = dual in-line package, the chip with two rows of pins. Insert it across the gap.

  • The notch (half circle) shows the direction. Hold the chip with the notch on the left: pin 1 is bottom-left, pins 1-7 run along the bottom from left to right, pins 8-14 along the top from right to left, so pin 14 is top-left.
  • Power: pin 14 → + rail with a male-male jumper, pin 7 → − rail (ground). All 14-pin CMOS 4000 chips are powered this way; 16-pin chips use pins 16 and 8.
  • CMOS 4081 (AND), 4071 (OR), 4070 (XOR) each have four 2-input logic gates. For the first gate, pins 1 and 2 are the inputs, pin 3 is the output. Connect an LED from pin 3 to ground.
  • Try it: connect the inputs to 0 (−) and 1 (+) with wires and see for which combinations the LED lights.
Recommended tools: (1) an IC pin straightener (about $10): new chips have their pins splayed out and do not fit the breadboard; (2) an IC extractor ($1.20) to pull chips out of the breadboard. Pulling with fingers bends and breaks the pins.
CMOS rules: unused inputs must not float, so tie them to − (or +); touch something grounded before you handle the chips; if a chip gets hot, unplug the power at once: it is probably inserted backwards.

5. Connect buttons

Do not add the pull-down resistors from old tutorials. Our button modules already include them.
  • A button module has three pins: + (VCC), OUT (the value, 0 or 1) and − (GND).
  • Use male-female jumpers: + to the + rail, OUT to the chip input, − to the − rail.
  • Not pressed: OUT = 0. Pressed: OUT = 1.
  • Use two buttons to drive the inputs of a logic gate (pins 1 and 2) and watch the LED on pin 3.

If something does not work

  • Nothing lights: is the power module's LED on? Is the module plugged in with + to +? Are both jumpers at 5 V? Are the rails split in the middle?
  • LEDs are dim: a 5 V USB-to-DC cable is used through the power module (3.5 V). Use the 9 V one.
  • The output flickers or changes by itself: an input is floating. Check the button's − wire and tie the unused inputs of the chip.
  • The chip is hot: it is inserted backwards (notch on the wrong side). Unplug the power.
  • An LED never lights: it is reversed: long leg to +.
