A teacher supplies a USB 5 V source and a multimeter. The buffer and capacitors are small additions to the source list: they make the logic signals independent of uncertain LED-module current. The teacher can prepare this observation stage once; students then plug signals into its inputs.

Use the specified modules. A bare two-pin LED is different from the 5 V current-limited LED in these notes. A bare switch is different from a resistor-equipped button module. A pull resistor establishes the released logic level; it does not remove contact bounce.

2. Understand the breadboard and power it

  1. Disconnect power while wiring. In the center area, groups of five holes share a contact. The center trench separates the two sides. Side power rails run lengthwise; some rails are split halfway. Use the meter’s continuity mode with power off to find any breaks.
  2. Seat the power module so its positive and ground pins match the board’s rail markings. Set each used output selector to 5 V. Bridge interrupted rails and connect grounds between boards when expanding.
  3. Connect USB 5 V source → boost cable marked 9 V → regulator barrel input → 5 V breadboard rails. Check the barrel polarity against the actual module. The 9 V stays on the regulator input.
  4. Before inserting a logic IC, measure the rail: red probe on +, black on GND. Aim for approximately 5.0 V; all shared HCT parts require 4.5–5.5 V. Turn power off again.
  5. Fit 10 µF between the rails, with correct polarity. Later place a 100 nF capacitor directly across each IC’s supply pins, as close to the IC as the layout allows.
The common power mix-up: a 5 V USB-to-barrel cable into this regulator may produce only about 3.5 V because of its voltage drop. Use the 9 V boost cable for this path. A direct, unregulated barrel breakout (LSSK row 58) instead needs a 5 V cable (row 59); do not feed it 9 V. An actual USB input on a regulator module is another option only if its documentation identifies it as an input.

3. Light one LED

With power off, connect the long anode lead to +5 V and the short cathode lead to GND. The flat side of the case usually marks the cathode; verify the supplied part if leads were trimmed. Switch on: it should light. Switch off before moving it. This direct supply test uses the LSSK current-limited 5 V LED, not a standard bare LED.

Checkpoint: the LED lights steadily and the supply still measures near 5 V.

4. Insert the chip across the center gap

5. Connect two buttons and the observer

  1. Use M–F jumpers to connect each module’s VCC to +5 V and GND to GND. Connect OUT from button A to CD4070 pin 1, and OUT from B to pin 2.
  2. Check each module with the meter: this guide assumes released ≈0 V and pressed ≈5 V. If a module is active-low, record the reversed meaning or choose the specified active-high variant. An analog resistor-ladder keypad cannot replace these independent outputs.
  3. Use the prepared observation stage below. Connect its first input to CD4070 pin 3. The extra buffer lets the logic chip drive a logic input while the buffer supplies LED current.
Teacher’s observation stage — exact connections

SN74HCT244N: pin 20 → +5 V; pin 10 → GND; enable pins 1 and 19 → GND; 100 nF across supply. First channel: pin 2 (input) ← CD4070 pin 3; pin 18 (output) → 2.2 kΩ → LED bar channel 0. Connect the bar’s common cathode to GND. Tie unused buffer inputs 4, 6, 8, 11, 13, 15, 17 to GND. Leave unused outputs disconnected.

For later experiments the eight input→output pairs are 2→18, 4→16, 6→14, 8→12, 11→9, 13→7, 15→5, 17→3. Each used output has its own 2.2 kΩ resistor before the corresponding LED channel. The bar must be the passive, common-cathode current-limited version. With a common-anode or transistor-input variant, use its documented interface instead.

The original sketches show LEDs directly on gates. A 5 V rating ensures an LED can be powered from 5 V; it does not prove a CD4000 output can drive its current while retaining a valid logic-high voltage. This buffer is the documented modification. TI buffer datasheet.

You have finished the first experiment when you can explain the four XOR results, find the chip’s power pins without guessing, and distinguish a logic input from an output.

7. Watch one bit of memory

Replace the gate with CD4013BE. Keep the LED observer. Add one assembled 555 clock module (LSSK row 54) and one SN74HCT14N clock conditioner with a 100 nF capacitor. Use two buttons: DATA and RESET. Set the clock to approximately 0.5–1 Hz.

The clock module shares the same 5 V supply and GND. HCT14’s two inversions preserve the clock polarity and restore full CMOS levels. Do not connect a raw mechanical button to a clock input; for manual sampling use the RC + Schmitt circuit in engineering notes.

  1. Press RESET, release it, then set DATA before a rising edge.
  2. After that edge, Q must equal the sampled DATA.
  3. Change DATA midway through the cycle: Q must hold until the next rising edge.
  4. Press RESET between edges: Q must clear immediately.

If something does not work

SymptomCheck
Nothing lightsSupply voltage and polarity, power switch, split rails, LED cathode, buffer enables.
A gate behaves randomlyFloating inputs, misplaced pins, button output polarity, loose wires and local decoupling.
Outputs change more than once per button pressContact bounce. Use conditioned manual sampling; an embedded pull resistor is not a debouncer.
Clock LED blinks but Q never changesCommon ground, HCT14 wiring, SET held low, RESET released, D stable before the rising edge.
A chip becomes warm or the rail voltage collapsesTurn power off; check rotated ICs, reversed supply and outputs accidentally joined together.

Where to go next

Try the counter, shift register and adder. For a seven-segment display, use a common cathode, a 4511, and seven separate 560 Ω resistors—one per segment. Then move to the arbiter, sequence FSM or FIFO project. These same structures form the bridge to SystemVerilog and FPGA exercises in BGM.
